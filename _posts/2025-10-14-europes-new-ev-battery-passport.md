From February 2027, every EV and industrial battery sold or used in the European Union must include a digital record called a battery passport. Accessed via a QR code on the unit, it will provide information on where the battery was manufactured, what materials were used, and the environmental and social impact of its production, including carbon emissions and labor practices throughout the supply chain.

This matters because it institutionalizes end-to-end transparency over the entire lifetime of a battery. Compositional and structural information enables safer dismantling and more reliable state-of-health assessments, allowing for more effective reuse and recycling. By making carbon and sourcing information accessible, the passport creates a common basis for procurement, due diligence and policy enforcement, shifting incentives toward traceable, lower-impact supply chains.

The regulation is ambitious, but it faces practical constraints. Aggregating verifiable data across multiple jurisdictions will be complex, and suppliers will resist sharing commercially sensitive details without robust confidentiality and IP protection. The compliance burden on smaller suppliers deserves careful consideration too, to prevent unfair disadvantages, while variability in carbon-reporting methodologies risks undermining comparability altogether. The regulation makes the market actor responsible for the passport, yet the practical handoffs at second life and waste disposal remain murky. Independent verification, clear data standards, and guidance for responsibility handover when batteries change owners will all be crucial.

The bottom line is that the battery passport is not a magic fix, but it is a significant shift, one that will change incentives and surface real problems. Early movers will win credibility and market access. Success depends on the upholding of standards, the verification of data, and incentives strong enough to get reluctant suppliers to share the truth.
